OxyContin: Complete Guide to Uses, Dosage, Side Effects & Risks

By Promedic Medical Team  |  August 5, 2026  |  12 min read

OxyContin is one of the most widely prescribed — and most widely discussed — opioid pain medications in the United States. Since its approval by the FDA in 1995, it has helped millions of patients manage severe, chronic pain while also becoming central to conversations about the opioid epidemic. Understanding what OxyContin is, how it works, when it is medically appropriate, and what risks it carries is essential for patients, caregivers, and healthcare professionals alike.

Important Medical Disclaimer: OxyContin is a Schedule II controlled substance available by prescription only. This article is for informational purposes only and does not constitute medical advice. Always consult a licensed healthcare provider before starting, stopping, or changing any prescription medication.
Key Takeaway: OxyContin (extended-release oxycodone) is prescribed for severe, around-the-clock pain requiring long-term opioid treatment. It carries a high risk of addiction, dependence, and overdose. It must only be used under close medical supervision with a valid prescription.

What Is OxyContin?

OxyContin is the brand name for oxycodone hydrochloride extended-release (ER) tablets, manufactured by Purdue Pharma. The active ingredient, oxycodone, is a semi-synthetic opioid derived from thebaine, an alkaloid found in the opium poppy plant. The "extended-release" formulation is the defining feature of OxyContin — unlike immediate-release oxycodone, which acts quickly and lasts 4 to 6 hours, OxyContin is designed to release medication slowly into the bloodstream over 12 hours.

OxyContin tablets are available in multiple strengths: 10 mg, 15 mg, 20 mg, 30 mg, 40 mg, 60 mg, and 80 mg. The 60 mg and 80 mg tablets are reserved for patients who are opioid-tolerant, meaning their bodies have adapted to regular opioid use. The 80 mg tablet is among the highest-dose oral opioid formulations available commercially.

In 2010, Purdue Pharma reformulated OxyContin with abuse-deterrent technology, making it harder to crush, snort, or dissolve. This reformulation has been associated with a reduction in certain patterns of misuse, though it did not eliminate the risks of addiction or overdose when taken orally as intended.

How Does OxyContin Work?

OxyContin works by binding to mu-opioid receptors in the brain, spinal cord, and peripheral tissues. When oxycodone activates these receptors, it reduces the perception of pain, decreases the emotional response to pain, and produces sedation. It also activates reward pathways in the brain, which is the primary driver of its addiction potential.

The extended-release mechanism works by using a polymer matrix that controls how quickly oxycodone is released. After swallowing a tablet, the outer layer dissolves quickly, releasing a small initial dose. The core then continues to release medication at a controlled rate for approximately 12 hours. This maintains relatively stable blood plasma levels of oxycodone, providing consistent pain relief without the peaks and troughs associated with immediate-release formulations.

This pharmacokinetic profile makes OxyContin suitable for patients who need continuous pain management — particularly those with cancer pain or severe chronic non-cancer pain — where stable drug levels are clinically important.

Medical Uses of OxyContin

The FDA-approved indication for OxyContin is the management of pain severe enough to require daily, around-the-clock, long-term opioid treatment, and for which alternative treatment options are inadequate. This is a deliberately narrow and specific indication. OxyContin is not intended for:

  • As-needed (PRN) pain relief
  • Mild to moderate pain
  • Short-term post-surgical pain
  • Patients who are not already opioid-tolerant (for doses above 40 mg)

Clinical conditions for which OxyContin may be prescribed include advanced cancer with severe pain, severe chronic back pain unresponsive to non-opioid therapies, severe neuropathic pain, and other conditions where the expected benefit outweighs the substantial risks. The prescribing decision must involve a careful individualized assessment by a qualified physician.

OxyContin Dosage and Administration

OxyContin dosing is highly individualized and must be determined by a prescribing physician based on pain severity, prior opioid exposure, and patient response. General dosing principles include:

  • Tablets should be swallowed whole — never crushed, chewed, broken, or dissolved
  • Doses are typically given every 12 hours
  • Starting doses for opioid-naive patients are typically 10 mg every 12 hours
  • Dose titration should be gradual, with reassessment at each step
  • Patients must be reassessed regularly to determine if opioid therapy remains appropriate
Critical Warning: Crushing, chewing, or dissolving OxyContin tablets causes rapid release of the full dose, which can cause fatal overdose. Never alter the tablet in any way.

Side Effects of OxyContin

Like all opioid medications, OxyContin is associated with a wide range of side effects. Some are predictable effects of opioid receptor activation, while others are idiosyncratic or dose-dependent.

Common Side Effects

  • Constipation (the most consistent and often persistent opioid side effect)
  • Nausea and vomiting, especially when first starting therapy
  • Drowsiness and sedation
  • Dizziness and lightheadedness
  • Dry mouth
  • Headache
  • Itching (pruritus)
  • Sweating

Serious Side Effects

  • Respiratory depression: Slow, shallow breathing is the most dangerous side effect and the primary cause of opioid overdose deaths. Risk is highest after dose increases, in opioid-naive patients, and when combined with other CNS depressants.
  • Hypotension: Significant drops in blood pressure, especially on standing (orthostatic hypotension).
  • Hormonal effects: Long-term use can suppress sex hormones, causing decreased libido, erectile dysfunction, and menstrual irregularities.
  • Immunosuppression: Chronic opioid use has been associated with impaired immune function.
  • Opioid-induced hyperalgesia: Paradoxically, long-term opioid use can increase pain sensitivity in some patients.
  • Cognitive impairment: Memory problems, difficulty concentrating, and slowed thinking.

Addiction, Dependence, and Withdrawal

OxyContin carries a black box warning — the FDA's most serious warning — for addiction, abuse, misuse, and potentially fatal respiratory depression. Physical dependence on oxycodone can develop within days to weeks of regular use, even when taken exactly as prescribed. Physical dependence is not the same as addiction, but it does mean the body has adapted to the drug's presence.

Addiction involves compulsive drug-seeking behavior despite harmful consequences. Risk factors for OxyContin addiction include a personal or family history of substance use disorder, history of mental health disorders (depression, anxiety, PTSD), history of trauma or adverse childhood experiences, and concurrent use of other psychoactive substances.

Withdrawal symptoms when stopping OxyContin abruptly include severe anxiety, muscle aches, insomnia, sweating, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, goosebumps, and intense drug cravings. Opioid withdrawal is rarely life-threatening in otherwise healthy adults, but it is extremely uncomfortable. OxyContin should never be stopped abruptly — dose tapering under medical supervision is essential.

Seeking Help: If you or someone you know is struggling with OxyContin misuse or addiction, the SAMHSA National Helpline provides free, confidential support 24/7: 1-800-662-4357.

Drug Interactions

OxyContin has clinically significant interactions with numerous medications and substances:

  • Benzodiazepines (Xanax, Valium, Klonopin): This combination is responsible for a large proportion of opioid overdose deaths. The FDA mandates a black box warning for this combination.
  • Alcohol: Dramatically increases CNS and respiratory depression. Can be fatal.
  • Other opioids: Additive respiratory depression risk.
  • MAO inhibitors (MAOIs): Contraindicated; can cause severe serotonin syndrome-like reactions.
  • CYP3A4 inhibitors (certain antifungals, antibiotics, HIV medications): Can increase oxycodone blood levels and risk of overdose.
  • CYP3A4 inducers (rifampin, carbamazepine): Can decrease oxycodone levels, reducing efficacy.
  • Muscle relaxants, sleep aids, antihistamines: Additive CNS depression.

Who Should Not Take OxyContin?

OxyContin is contraindicated in patients with:

  • Significant respiratory depression or compromised airway
  • Acute or severe bronchial asthma without resuscitative equipment
  • Known or suspected gastrointestinal obstruction (including paralytic ileus)
  • Hypersensitivity to oxycodone or any component of the formulation
  • Concurrent use of MAO inhibitors (within 14 days)

OxyContin should be used with extreme caution — or not at all — in elderly or frail patients, patients with compromised pulmonary function, patients with history of substance use disorder, patients with head injuries or raised intracranial pressure, and patients with hepatic or renal impairment.

OxyContin vs Immediate-Release Oxycodone

A common question is the difference between OxyContin and immediate-release (IR) oxycodone products. The key differences are as follows: OxyContin is designed for around-the-clock pain control with 12-hour dosing intervals, while IR oxycodone is used for acute or breakthrough pain with dosing every 4 to 6 hours. OxyContin should not be used as a supplemental dose for breakthrough pain — a separate IR opioid is typically prescribed for that purpose.

Overdose: Signs, Response, and Prevention

OxyContin overdose is a life-threatening emergency. Because each tablet contains a large amount of oxycodone, a single extra dose or accidental ingestion by a child can be fatal. Signs of opioid overdose include extreme drowsiness or unresponsiveness, slow, shallow, or stopped breathing, blue or purple lips and fingertips (cyanosis), pinpoint pupils, gurgling or choking sounds, and limpness.

If you suspect an overdose, call 911 immediately. Administer naloxone (Narcan) if available — it reverses opioid overdose and is available without a prescription at most pharmacies in the United States. The effects of naloxone are temporary, so emergency medical care is still essential even if the person appears to recover.

Prevention strategies include storing OxyContin in a locked, secure location away from children and other household members; never sharing medication; disposing of unused tablets at an authorized medication drop-box; and having naloxone available in the home.

Safe Storage and Disposal of OxyContin

Proper storage and disposal of OxyContin are critical public health measures. Store all oxycontin tablets at room temperature, away from moisture and heat, in a child-resistant container in a locked cabinet. Unused OxyContin should be disposed of promptly — do not keep medication "just in case." The DEA operates authorized collection sites at many pharmacies. If no collection site is available, the FDA-approved drug disposal method is to mix the tablets with an undesirable substance (like used coffee grounds or dirt) in a sealed bag and place in household trash.
