Oxycodone: Complete Guide to Uses, Dosage, Side Effects & Safety

By Promedic Medical Team  |  August 5, 2026  |  12 min read

Oxycodone is one of the most commonly prescribed opioid analgesics in the United States. It is used to treat moderate to severe pain across a wide range of medical conditions and is available in several formulations. Understanding how oxycodone works, what it is used for, its side effects, and the significant risks it carries is essential for patients receiving this medication and their families.

Important Medical Disclaimer: Oxycodone is a Schedule II controlled substance available by prescription only. This article is for informational purposes only and does not constitute medical advice. Always consult a licensed healthcare provider before starting, stopping, or changing any prescription medication.
Key Takeaway: Oxycodone is a powerful opioid used for moderate to severe pain. It requires a valid prescription and carries significant risks including addiction, dependence, and potentially fatal respiratory depression, especially when combined with alcohol or other CNS depressants.

What Is Oxycodone?

Oxycodone is a semi-synthetic opioid analgesic — a drug derived from thebaine, a naturally occurring alkaloid of the opium poppy. It was first synthesized in 1916 in Germany as part of research into opioid compounds with better tolerability than morphine. Oxycodone was introduced into clinical medicine in the United States in 1939 and has been widely used since.

The drug is classified as a Schedule II controlled substance under the Controlled Substances Act, indicating that it has accepted medical uses but a high potential for abuse that may lead to severe psychological or physical dependence. Prescribing, dispensing, and storing oxycodone are subject to strict federal and state regulations.

Oxycodone is available in multiple formulations:

  • Immediate-release (IR) tablets — typically 5 mg, 10 mg, 15 mg, 20 mg, 30 mg — for acute and breakthrough pain, lasting 4 to 6 hours
  • Extended-release (ER) tablets — sold under the brand name OxyContin — for around-the-clock pain management, lasting approximately 12 hours
  • Combination products — oxycodone paired with acetaminophen (Percocet, Roxicet) or aspirin (Percodan) for enhanced analgesia
  • Oral solution — for patients who cannot swallow tablets

How Does Oxycodone Work?

Oxycodone's primary mechanism of action is agonism at mu-opioid receptors (MOR) throughout the central nervous system. When oxycodone binds to these G-protein coupled receptors, it triggers a cascade of intracellular events that reduce neuronal excitability and decrease the transmission of pain signals. This results in analgesia (pain relief), sedation, and euphoria.

The drug also acts at kappa and delta opioid receptors, contributing to its overall pharmacological profile. In addition to pain relief, opioid receptor activation produces several effects that account for both its therapeutic benefits and side effects: slowed gastrointestinal motility (leading to constipation), suppression of the cough reflex, respiratory depression, and activation of the mesolimbic dopamine system (the brain's reward circuit).

Oxycodone is well-absorbed orally, with bioavailability of approximately 60 to 87%. It is metabolized primarily by the liver enzyme CYP3A4 and secondarily by CYP2D6. The primary active metabolite is oxymorphone, which also has analgesic properties. Oxycodone has a half-life of approximately 3 to 4 hours for the immediate-release form.

Medical Uses of Oxycodone

Oxycodone is prescribed for moderate to severe pain in a variety of clinical contexts. Common indications include:

  • Post-surgical pain — short-term management following procedures where non-opioid analgesics are insufficient
  • Cancer pain — one of the most important and well-established uses of oxycodone, particularly for patients with moderate to severe pain from malignancy
  • Severe acute injuries — fractures, trauma, burns
  • Chronic non-cancer pain — severe back pain, neuropathic pain, arthritis — though guidelines recommend non-opioid treatments first and careful risk-benefit assessment before prescribing opioids for chronic non-cancer pain
  • Palliative care — pain management at end of life

Oxycodone should only be prescribed when non-opioid and non-pharmacological pain management options have proven insufficient. Current CDC guidelines on opioid prescribing for chronic pain emphasize using the lowest effective dose for the shortest duration necessary, with regular reassessment of risks and benefits.

Oxycodone Dosage Information

Oxycodone dosing must be individualized based on pain severity, patient opioid tolerance, kidney and liver function, age, and weight. Standard dosing for immediate-release oxycodone in opioid-naive adults is typically 5 to 15 mg every 4 to 6 hours as needed. Doses should be increased only under medical supervision, titrating to adequate pain relief while monitoring for adverse effects.

Side Effects of Oxycodone

Oxycodone shares the side effect profile common to all opioid analgesics. Side effects can be broadly categorized as common and expected, or serious and potentially dangerous.

Common Side Effects

  • Constipation — the most universal and persistent opioid side effect, affecting virtually all patients. Unlike other side effects, tolerance to opioid-induced constipation does not develop. Prophylactic laxative therapy is routinely recommended.
  • Nausea and vomiting — common on initiation; often improves with continued use
  • Drowsiness and sedation — particularly pronounced initially and after dose increases
  • Dizziness and lightheadedness — falls risk, especially in elderly patients
  • Dry mouth
  • Sweating
  • Itching (pruritus) — more common with IV opioids but can occur with oral formulations
  • Headache

Serious Side Effects

  • Respiratory depression — the most dangerous acute side effect. Oxycodone reduces the sensitivity of the respiratory center in the brainstem to rising CO₂ levels, leading to slower, shallower breathing. This is the primary mechanism of opioid overdose death.
  • CNS depression — impaired cognition, psychomotor slowing, and altered consciousness
  • Hypotension — particularly when standing up (orthostatic hypotension)
  • Hormonal disruption — long-term use suppresses the hypothalamic-pituitary axis, reducing testosterone, estrogen, and cortisol levels. This can cause sexual dysfunction, reduced fertility, weight gain, and fatigue.
  • Immunosuppression — evidence suggests chronic opioid use impairs immune function
  • Opioid-induced hyperalgesia (OIH) — paradoxical increased pain sensitivity with long-term use in some patients

Oxycodone vs Hydrocodone: Key Differences

Oxycodone and hydrocodone are frequently compared as they are the two most commonly prescribed opioid analgesics in the United States. Both are Schedule II substances with similar mechanisms of action. Key differences include:

  • Potency: Oxycodone is approximately 1.5 times more potent than hydrocodone on a milligram-per-milligram basis, though clinical equivalence varies by patient.
  • Formulations: Oxycodone is available as a single-ingredient product; hydrocodone is most commonly prescribed in combination with acetaminophen (Vicodin, Norco, Lortab).
  • Availability: Both are available in immediate-release and extended-release formulations.
  • Metabolism: Oxycodone is primarily metabolized by CYP3A4; hydrocodone by CYP2D6 and CYP3A4.
  • Side effects: Both have highly similar side effect profiles. Hydrocodone combination products carry additional hepatotoxicity risk from acetaminophen.

Addiction, Physical Dependence, and Withdrawal

Oxycodone has a high potential for abuse and addiction. Physical dependence — where the body requires the drug to function normally — can develop within days to weeks of regular use, even at therapeutic doses and when taken exactly as prescribed. This is a neurobiological adaptation, not a character flaw or personal failure.

Addiction involves compulsive drug-seeking and use despite awareness of harmful consequences. It is a chronic neurological condition influenced by genetic, psychological, and social factors. Risk factors for opioid addiction include a personal or family history of substance use disorder, co-occurring mental health conditions (depression, anxiety, PTSD, bipolar disorder), history of childhood trauma or adverse experiences, and age of first exposure (younger exposure increases risk).

Opioid withdrawal occurs when oxycodone is stopped abruptly or dose is reduced significantly in a physically dependent person. Symptoms typically begin 8 to 24 hours after the last dose (sooner for IR formulations) and include severe anxiety and restlessness, muscle aches and cramps, insomnia, sweating and goosebumps, runny nose and watery eyes, nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea, and intense drug cravings. Oxycodone should never be stopped abruptly — tapering under medical supervision is essential.

Treatment Resources: Medication-assisted treatment (MAT) with buprenorphine or methadone is the gold standard for opioid use disorder. If you or someone you know needs help, contact SAMHSA: 1-800-662-4357 (free, confidential, 24/7).

Critical Drug Interactions

Oxycodone has life-threatening interactions with several substances. Inform all healthcare providers about every medication and supplement you take:

  • Alcohol — additive CNS and respiratory depression; can be fatal even at moderate alcohol intake
  • Benzodiazepines (diazepam, alprazolam, lorazepam, clonazepam) — the FDA requires a black box warning for this combination; responsible for a large percentage of opioid overdose deaths
  • Other opioids — additive respiratory depression
  • Sleep medications (zolpidem, eszopiclone) — additive CNS depression
  • Muscle relaxants (cyclobenzaprine, carisoprodol) — additive CNS depression
  • MAO inhibitors — contraindicated within 14 days; can cause severe serotonin syndrome
  • CYP3A4 inhibitors (clarithromycin, itraconazole, ritonavir) — increase oxycodone levels, raising overdose risk
  • CYP3A4 inducers (rifampin, carbamazepine, phenytoin) — decrease oxycodone levels, reducing efficacy

Special Populations

Certain groups require particular caution with oxycodone:

  • Elderly patients — increased sensitivity to CNS effects, higher fall risk, reduced renal clearance; lower starting doses required
  • Patients with hepatic impairment — reduced metabolism; dose reduction and careful monitoring required
  • Patients with renal impairment — accumulation of active metabolites; dose adjustment needed
  • Pregnant women — oxycodone crosses the placenta and can cause neonatal opioid withdrawal syndrome; use only if benefits clearly outweigh risks
  • Patients with COPD or sleep apnea — heightened respiratory depression risk; extreme caution required
  • Children — not approved for use in children; accidental ingestion can be fatal

Recognizing and Responding to Oxycodone Overdose

Oxycodone overdose is a medical emergency. Knowing the warning signs and how to respond can save a life. Signs of overdose include unresponsiveness or extreme difficulty waking, very slow, shallow, or stopped breathing, gurgling or choking sounds, blue or grayish lips and fingertips, pinpoint (very small) pupils, and limp body.

If you suspect an overdose: call 911 immediately; try to wake the person by calling their name and rubbing knuckles on their sternum; administer naloxone (Narcan) if available — it reverses opioid effects and is available without a prescription at pharmacies in most US states; place the person in the recovery position (on their side) if breathing; stay with them until help arrives. Naloxone wears off in 30 to 90 minutes, so even if the person appears to recover, emergency care is essential.
