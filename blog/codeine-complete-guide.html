Codeine: Complete Guide to Uses, Dosage, Side Effects & Risks

By Promedic Medical Team  |  August 21, 2026  |  11 min read

Codeine is one of the oldest and most widely recognized opioid drugs in the world. Present in opium at concentrations of 0.7–2.5%, it has been used medicinally for over 170 years as a cough suppressant and mild-to-moderate pain reliever. Despite being one of the weaker opioids, codeine has a complex pharmacology, a significant addiction potential, and a notable safety history — including a critical and often misunderstood risk in certain patient populations that led to major prescribing restrictions in the past decade.

Important Medical Disclaimer: Codeine is a Schedule II–V controlled substance (depending on formulation) available only with a prescription in the United States. This article is for educational purposes only. Always consult a licensed healthcare provider before taking any opioid medication.
Key Takeaway: Codeine is a prodrug — it must be converted to morphine by the liver enzyme CYP2D6 to produce its analgesic effects. This means its effectiveness and safety vary dramatically based on genetics. It is FDA-banned in children under 12 for cough and cold use due to fatal overdose risk in ultra-rapid metabolizers.

What Is Codeine?

Codeine is a naturally occurring opioid alkaloid found in the opium poppy, typically present in small amounts (less than 2% of opium). While some codeine is derived directly from opium, most pharmaceutical codeine is semi-synthetically produced from morphine. Chemically, it is 3-methylmorphine — a methylated form of morphine.

Codeine is classified under the Controlled Substances Act as follows:

  • Schedule II — single-ingredient codeine products (e.g., pure codeine tablets)
  • Schedule III — combination products with up to 90 mg codeine per dosage unit (e.g., Tylenol with Codeine #3)
  • Schedule V — cough preparations with less than 200 mg codeine per 100 mL (e.g., some cough syrups)

Common brand names include Tylenol with Codeine (acetaminophen/codeine), Fioricet with Codeine (butalbital/acetaminophen/caffeine/codeine), Promethazine VC with Codeine (a cough syrup), and various generic formulations.

How Codeine Works: The Prodrug Mechanism

Codeine is classified as a prodrug — it has minimal direct opioid activity. Its analgesic and euphoric effects depend entirely on its conversion to morphine by the liver enzyme CYP2D6 (cytochrome P450 2D6). Approximately 5–10% of a codeine dose is O-demethylated to morphine, which then binds to mu-opioid receptors to produce pain relief. A smaller fraction is N-demethylated to norcodeine and converted to norhydrocodone.

This prodrug mechanism has profound implications for both efficacy and safety:

  • Poor metabolizers (CYP2D6 deficient, ~7–10% of whites, ~1–2% of Asians) — convert virtually no codeine to morphine; experience little or no pain relief or cough suppression
  • Extensive metabolizers (most of the population) — convert a normal amount; standard expected response
  • Ultra-rapid metabolizers (CYP2D6 duplications, ~1–7% of Europeans, up to 29% of North Africans) — convert codeine to morphine extremely rapidly and in large amounts; risk of life-threatening morphine toxicity even at standard doses

Medical Uses of Codeine

Pain Management

Codeine is used for mild to moderate pain that is not adequately controlled by non-opioid analgesics. Common formulations pair codeine with acetaminophen (Tylenol with Codeine #2: 15 mg codeine; #3: 30 mg codeine; #4: 60 mg codeine) or with aspirin. The typical adult oral dose for pain is 15–60 mg every 4–6 hours, not exceeding 360 mg per day.

Cough Suppression

Codeine has been used for over a century as an antitussive (cough suppressant). It acts in the brainstem's cough center to raise the threshold for cough reflex activation. Prescription cough syrups containing 10–20 mg codeine per dose are used for severe, unproductive cough. The FDA, however, has significantly restricted this use — codeine is now contraindicated in anyone under 18 for cough and cold indications following several pediatric deaths.

Diarrhea

Like all opioids, codeine slows intestinal motility. Some formulations (e.g., paregoric — camphorated tincture of opium) have historically been used for diarrhea, though this use is now largely replaced by loperamide (Imodium), which provides the intestinal effects of opioids without significant CNS penetration.

The Pediatric Safety Crisis and FDA Restrictions

The single most important safety development in codeine's recent history is the series of FDA actions restricting its use in children and adolescents. Between 1969 and 2012, the FDA received reports of at least 21 pediatric deaths and 3 cases of life-threatening respiratory depression linked to codeine use in children — most occurring in post-tonsillectomy patients who were CYP2D6 ultra-rapid metabolizers.

As a result:

  • 2013: FDA added a Black Box Warning contraindicting codeine after tonsillectomy/adenoidectomy in children
  • 2017: FDA contraindicated codeine for cough and cold in ALL children under 12, and warned against use in children 12–18 who are obese, have sleep apnea, or lung disease
  • 2018: FDA recommended against use in breastfeeding mothers (morphine in breast milk can harm nursing infants who are ultra-rapid metabolizers)

Many countries outside the US have implemented similar or stricter restrictions on codeine use in pediatric populations.

"Lean" and Codeine Misuse: A Public Health Crisis

Codeine cough syrup has become a significant substance of abuse, particularly in the United States and among younger demographics. The concoction known as "lean," "purple drank," or "sizzurp" typically consists of prescription-strength codeine/promethazine cough syrup mixed with a carbonated soft drink (often Sprite or Mountain Dew) and sometimes candy.

The practice has been glamorized in hip-hop music and culture since at least the 1990s and has contributed to numerous overdose deaths, including prominent musicians and celebrities. Promethazine (an antihistamine with CNS-depressant properties) potentiates codeine's respiratory depression, making the combination particularly dangerous. Mixing lean with alcohol, benzodiazepines, or other opioids dramatically increases the risk of fatal respiratory arrest.

In response, several states have placed codeine cough syrups behind the pharmacy counter or restricted their sale even where Schedule V products would otherwise be available without a prescription.

Side Effects of Codeine

  • Constipation — universal, as with all opioids
  • Nausea and vomiting
  • Drowsiness and sedation
  • Dizziness and lightheadedness
  • Itching (histamine release is more pronounced with codeine than most other opioids)
  • Dry mouth
  • Respiratory depression — most serious, especially in ultra-rapid metabolizers or when combined with other CNS depressants
  • Addiction and physical dependence with regular use
  • Tolerance — requiring higher doses over time for the same effect

Drug Interactions

  • CYP2D6 inhibitors (fluoxetine, paroxetine, bupropion, quinidine) — block conversion to morphine, dramatically reducing efficacy; create unpredictable analgesic response
  • CYP3A4 inhibitors (ketoconazole, erythromycin, grapefruit juice) — reduce codeine metabolism, potentially increasing codeine and metabolite concentrations
  • Benzodiazepines and alcohol — highly dangerous; potentiates CNS and respiratory depression
  • MAO inhibitors — contraindicated; risk of severe adverse reactions
  • Promethazine — often combined in cough syrups; additive CNS depression
  • Serotonergic medications — serotonin syndrome risk with SSRIs, SNRIs, and tramadol

Codeine Addiction and Withdrawal

Despite being one of the weaker opioids, codeine carries real addiction potential. Physical dependence develops with regular use, and abrupt discontinuation produces withdrawal symptoms including anxiety, restlessness, sweating, muscle aching, insomnia, nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea. Because codeine is partly converted to morphine, codeine dependence is essentially a partial morphine dependence.

Treatment of codeine use disorder follows the same principles as other opioid use disorders: medications for opioid use disorder (MOUD) such as buprenorphine/naloxone (Suboxone), methadone, or extended-release naltrexone (Vivitrol), combined with behavioral therapy, produce the best long-term outcomes.

Help Is Available: SAMHSA National Helpline: 1-800-662-4357 — free, confidential, 24/7 treatment referral service.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is codeine used for?
Codeine is used to treat mild to moderate pain and as a prescription cough suppressant. It is available in combination with acetaminophen for pain, and in cough syrups. It must be converted to morphine by the liver to work.
Is codeine stronger than hydrocodone?
No. Hydrocodone is significantly more potent. Approximately 200 mg of codeine equals 30 mg of morphine in analgesic effect, while 30 mg of hydrocodone equals 30 mg of morphine. This makes hydrocodone about 6–7 times more potent than codeine by weight.
Why is codeine dangerous in children?
Children who are CYP2D6 ultra-rapid metabolizers convert codeine to morphine extremely rapidly. This can cause life-threatening respiratory depression even at normal doses. The FDA banned codeine for use in children under 12 for cough and cold after multiple pediatric deaths were reported.
How long does codeine stay in your system?
Codeine has a half-life of 2.5–3.5 hours. Detection times: urine 1–3 days; blood up to 24 hours; saliva 1–4 days; hair up to 90 days.
Is "lean" (codeine cough syrup) dangerous?
Yes. Lean is a dangerous recreational concoction of codeine cough syrup and soda that has caused numerous overdose deaths. The combination with promethazine (often present in prescription cough syrup) dramatically potentiates respiratory depression. Mixing with alcohol or benzodiazepines can be fatal.
