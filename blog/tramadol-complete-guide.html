Tramadol: Complete Guide to Uses, Dosage, Side Effects & Risks

By Promedic Medical Team  |  August 21, 2026  |  12 min read

Tramadol is one of the most widely prescribed pain medications in the world — and one of the most misunderstood. Unlike conventional opioids, tramadol works through two distinct mechanisms: weak mu-opioid receptor agonism and inhibition of serotonin and norepinephrine reuptake. This dual mechanism gives tramadol a unique clinical profile, making it useful for certain pain types while also creating risks not seen with other opioids — particularly seizures and serotonin syndrome.

Medical Disclaimer: Tramadol is a Schedule IV controlled substance requiring a prescription. This article is for educational purposes only. Consult a licensed healthcare provider before using tramadol.
Key Takeaway: Tramadol is an atypical opioid that also inhibits serotonin and norepinephrine reuptake. This makes it effective for neuropathic and musculoskeletal pain, but creates unique risks — especially seizures (dose-dependent), serotonin syndrome (when combined with antidepressants), and unpredictable efficacy based on CYP2D6 genetics.

What Is Tramadol?

Tramadol is a centrally-acting synthetic analgesic that was developed in Germany by Grünenthal GmbH and introduced in 1977. Initially not classified as a controlled substance due to its believed low abuse potential, tramadol was placed on Schedule IV of the Controlled Substances Act in 2014 after evidence of significant recreational misuse, dependence, and overdose deaths accumulated.

Brand names include Ultram (immediate-release), Ultram ER and ConZip (extended-release), and Ultracet (tramadol/acetaminophen combination). Generic tramadol hydrochloride is widely available in 50 mg immediate-release tablets and 100/150/200/300 mg extended-release tablets.

How Tramadol Works: Dual Mechanism of Action

Tramadol's analgesic effect comes from two distinct pharmacological actions that work synergistically:

1. Opioid Receptor Agonism (Prodrug Component)

Like codeine, tramadol is partly a prodrug. The liver enzyme CYP2D6 converts tramadol to its active metabolite O-desmethyltramadol (M1), which has significantly higher mu-opioid receptor affinity than tramadol itself (200-fold greater). This M1 metabolite contributes the majority of tramadol's opioid analgesic effect. As with codeine, CYP2D6 genetic variability profoundly affects tramadol's efficacy: poor metabolizers get little benefit; ultra-rapid metabolizers may experience unexpected opioid toxicity.

2. Serotonin and Norepinephrine Reuptake Inhibition

Independently of its opioid action, tramadol inhibits the reuptake of serotonin and norepinephrine in synapses — the same mechanism as antidepressants like duloxetine (Cymbalta) and venlafaxine (Effexor). This monoaminergic mechanism activates descending pain-inhibiting pathways from the brainstem, contributing to analgesia for neuropathic and chronic pain conditions. It also explains why tramadol has some utility in fibromyalgia and depression, and why it carries serious serotonin syndrome risk.

Medical Uses of Tramadol

  • Moderate to moderately severe acute pain: Post-surgical, dental, musculoskeletal injury
  • Chronic non-cancer pain: Osteoarthritis, low back pain, fibromyalgia — tramadol's dual mechanism makes it particularly useful for mixed pain syndromes
  • Neuropathic pain: Diabetic peripheral neuropathy, post-herpetic neuralgia — the norepinephrine reuptake component enhances efficacy for nerve pain
  • Off-label: Depression and anxiety — tramadol's monoaminergic properties produce antidepressant effects; some evidence supports use in treatment-resistant depression
  • Off-label: Premature ejaculation — serotonergic effects delay ejaculation; studied in clinical trials but not FDA-approved for this use

Unique Risks of Tramadol

Seizures

Tramadol significantly lowers the seizure threshold, and seizures are a recognized adverse effect, particularly at high doses. Risk factors for tramadol-related seizures include: dose above 400 mg/day; personal or family history of epilepsy or seizure disorders; concurrent use of medications that lower seizure threshold (SSRIs, TCAs, MAOIs, bupropion, certain antibiotics like quinolones); and metabolic disturbances. Tramadol is generally contraindicated in patients with seizure disorders.

Serotonin Syndrome

Because tramadol inhibits serotonin reuptake, combining it with other serotonergic drugs can trigger serotonin syndrome — a potentially life-threatening toxidrome characterized by the triad of: mental status changes (agitation, confusion), autonomic instability (fever, rapid heart rate, labile blood pressure), and neuromuscular abnormalities (tremor, myoclonus, hyperreflexia, rigidity). Severe serotonin syndrome can cause hyperthermia, rhabdomyolysis, acute kidney injury, and death.

Serotonin-increasing medications to avoid with tramadol include: all SSRIs (fluoxetine, sertraline, escitalopram, etc.), SNRIs (venlafaxine, duloxetine), MAO inhibitors (contraindicated within 14 days), triptans (sumatriptan, etc.), linezolid, methylene blue, lithium, St. John's Wort, and other opioids with serotonergic properties (meperidine, fentanyl, tapentadol).

Critical Drug Interaction: Never combine tramadol with MAO inhibitors. This combination can cause hypertensive crisis, serotonin syndrome, and death. A 14-day washout period is required after stopping an MAOI before starting tramadol.

Suicidal Risk

The FDA requires antidepressant Black Box Warnings to be applied to tramadol as well — due to its serotonergic activity — warning about increased risk of suicidal thinking and behavior in children, adolescents, and young adults (under 25). Additionally, tramadol is sometimes associated with increased suicide risk in patients already at risk, and overdose with tramadol has been used as a method of self-harm.

Side Effects of Tramadol

  • Nausea and vomiting (very common at initiation — more so than other opioids due to serotonin effects)
  • Dizziness and vertigo
  • Constipation (less severe than with classical opioids)
  • Headache
  • Drowsiness
  • Dry mouth
  • Sweating
  • Insomnia
  • Seizures (dose-dependent)
  • Serotonin syndrome (with drug interactions)
  • Respiratory depression (less severe than classical opioids at therapeutic doses; severe in overdose)

Tramadol Dependence and Withdrawal

Despite its Schedule IV classification — implying lower abuse potential — tramadol produces both physical and psychological dependence with regular use. Tramadol withdrawal is notably more complex than classical opioid withdrawal because it involves both opioid withdrawal symptoms (anxiety, muscle aching, sweating, diarrhea) AND atypical symptoms related to serotonin rebound: anxiety, panic attacks, hallucinations, and paresthesias (pins and needles). This serotonin-discontinuation component is sometimes called "discontinuation syndrome" and is not reversed by naloxone.

Standard opioid use disorder treatments (buprenorphine, methadone) address the opioid component of tramadol dependence but may not fully address the serotonergic withdrawal symptoms. Medical supervision during tramadol tapering is strongly recommended.

Help Is Available: SAMHSA National Helpline: 1-800-662-4357 — free, confidential, 24/7 substance use disorder treatment referral.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is tramadol used for?
Tramadol treats moderate to moderately severe pain including acute injuries, post-surgical pain, chronic musculoskeletal pain, osteoarthritis, and neuropathic pain. Its dual mechanism (opioid + SNRI) makes it particularly useful for mixed pain types.
Is tramadol a strong opioid?
Tramadol is considered a weak to moderate opioid. About 300 mg of tramadol provides equivalent analgesia to 30 mg of oral morphine. It is Schedule IV — a lower controlled substance classification than Schedule II opioids like oxycodone and morphine.
Can tramadol cause seizures?
Yes. Tramadol lowers the seizure threshold, especially at higher doses, in people with a seizure history, and when combined with SSRIs or other seizure-threshold-lowering medications. Risk is significantly elevated in overdose.
What is serotonin syndrome from tramadol?
Serotonin syndrome is a dangerous reaction caused by excess serotonin. Because tramadol inhibits serotonin reuptake, combining it with SSRIs, SNRIs, MAOIs, or triptans can trigger this syndrome — characterized by agitation, fever, rapid heart rate, muscle rigidity, and potentially death.
How long does tramadol stay in your system?
Tramadol has a half-life of 5–6 hours. Detection: urine 1–4 days; blood 24–48 hours; saliva 1–4 days; hair up to 90 days.
