Methadone: Complete Guide to Uses, Dosage, Risks & Opioid Treatment

By Promedic Medical Team  |  August 21, 2026  |  13 min read

Methadone is one of the most pharmacologically complex and clinically valuable opioids in medicine. Unique among opioids, it serves two entirely distinct medical roles: as a treatment for moderate-to-severe chronic pain, and as the cornerstone pharmacotherapy for opioid use disorder (OUD) through methadone maintenance treatment (MMT). Its dual utility, combined with extremely long and unpredictable half-life, make it one of the most powerful yet dangerous analgesics available — requiring careful prescriber expertise and patient monitoring.

Medical Disclaimer: Methadone is a Schedule II controlled substance. For pain, it requires a standard DEA prescription. For OUD treatment, it must be dispensed through a federally certified Opioid Treatment Program (OTP). This article is for educational purposes only.
Key Takeaway: Methadone's greatest clinical advantage is its extremely long half-life (24–36+ hours), which allows once-daily dosing and prevents withdrawal in OUD treatment. Its greatest danger is that same prolonged half-life — methadone can accumulate and cause delayed respiratory depression days after a dose change, making it responsible for a disproportionate share of opioid overdose deaths relative to prescriptions written.

What Is Methadone?

Methadone is a fully synthetic opioid analgesic first developed in Germany in 1937 and approved for use in the United States in 1947. Unlike morphine, oxycodone, and other opioids derived from or structurally related to the opium poppy, methadone is entirely synthetic. Brand names include Dolophine (for pain) and Methadose or Diskets (for OUD treatment). Generic methadone hydrochloride tablets (5 mg, 10 mg, 40 mg dispersible) and oral solution (5 mg/5 mL, 10 mg/5 mL) are widely used.

Methadone is a Schedule II controlled substance — the same schedule as oxycodone, morphine, and fentanyl — reflecting its high potential for abuse and severe dependence.

How Methadone Works

Methadone produces analgesia through multiple mechanisms that distinguish it from other opioids:

  • Mu-opioid receptor agonism: Like all opioids, methadone activates mu-opioid receptors to produce analgesia, sedation, and respiratory depression.
  • NMDA receptor antagonism: Methadone blocks N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptors, which are involved in central sensitization and opioid tolerance. This property may make methadone particularly effective for neuropathic pain and may help prevent or reverse opioid tolerance — one reason it is often used when patients fail other opioids.
  • Serotonin and norepinephrine reuptake inhibition: Weak monoaminergic activity contributes additional analgesic effects for some pain types.
  • Delta opioid receptor agonism: Additional analgesic contribution.

Methadone exists as a racemic mixture of two enantiomers: R-methadone (the active opioid enantiomer) and S-methadone (which contributes NMDA antagonism and QTc prolongation). The ratio affects its pharmacological profile relative to single-enantiomer preparations available in some countries.

Methadone's Extraordinarily Complex Pharmacokinetics

Methadone's pharmacokinetics are notoriously unpredictable and require expert clinical management:

  • Half-life: 24 to 36 hours — but this range can span from 8 hours to 59 hours across individuals. This variability is the primary source of methadone's danger.
  • Oral bioavailability: 36–100% (highly variable between individuals)
  • Time to peak effect: Pain relief occurs in 30 minutes to 1 hour; full steady-state plasma concentration not achieved for 3–5 days after dose initiation or change
  • Accumulation risk: Because methadone accumulates over days, a patient may feel fine after the first few doses and then develop life-threatening respiratory depression on days 3–5 as plasma levels peak

This accumulation pattern means that dose escalation must be done very slowly — typically no more frequently than every 5–7 days for pain patients — under close supervision. This is why methadone initiation for pain should only be done by clinicians experienced with its use, and why rapid dose changes should be avoided.

Medical Uses of Methadone

1. Opioid Use Disorder (MAT/MMT)

Methadone maintenance treatment is one of the most evidence-based treatments for opioid use disorder, with decades of research demonstrating significant reductions in illicit opioid use, criminal activity, HIV transmission, and overdose mortality. Methadone for OUD can only be dispensed through federally certified Opioid Treatment Programs (OTPs) — specialized clinics that provide daily supervised dosing, counseling, and medical monitoring. Starting doses are typically 20–30 mg/day, titrated slowly over weeks to months to a therapeutic maintenance dose (often 60–120 mg/day).

2. Chronic Severe Pain

Methadone is used as an analgesic for moderate to severe chronic pain, particularly when patients have failed other opioids (due to inadequate relief or intolerable side effects). Its NMDA antagonism may provide advantages in neuropathic pain and hyperalgesic states. Pain dosing is different from OUD dosing — typically lower and given 2–3 times daily rather than once daily. Methadone for pain requires a standard DEA-licensed prescriber; it does not require OTP certification.

3. Cancer Pain

Methadone is used in palliative and oncology settings, particularly for refractory cancer pain. Its low cost, multiple mechanisms, and effectiveness after opioid rotation (switching from other opioids) make it valuable in advanced cancer patients who have developed tolerance or side effects to other opioids.

QTc Prolongation: The Cardiac Risk

Methadone prolongs the cardiac QT interval — a measure of ventricular repolarization time on an electrocardiogram. Prolonged QTc increases the risk of a potentially fatal ventricular arrhythmia called Torsades de pointes. This risk is dose-dependent (most significant at doses above 100 mg/day) and is amplified by other QT-prolonging medications and by electrolyte abnormalities (low potassium or magnesium).

Clinical guidelines recommend baseline ECG before initiating methadone and repeat ECGs at dose thresholds. QTc above 500 ms warrants dose reduction or switching to an alternative opioid.

Always consult a palliative care or pain specialist for opioid rotation to methadone.

Drug Interactions

  • QT-prolonging medications — antipsychotics, certain antibiotics (azithromycin, fluoroquinolones), antidepressants, antifungals: increased Torsades risk
  • CYP3A4 and CYP2D6 inducers (rifampin, phenytoin, carbamazepine, St. John's Wort) — dramatically reduce methadone levels; can precipitate withdrawal
  • CYP3A4 inhibitors (azole antifungals, certain HIV antiretrovirals) — increase methadone levels; risk of toxicity
  • Benzodiazepines and alcohol — significantly increases respiratory depression risk; historically responsible for many MMT patient deaths
  • HIV antiretrovirals — many ARV drugs significantly alter methadone metabolism; close monitoring required for HIV-positive MMT patients

Overdose and Safety

Methadone is responsible for a disproportionate share of prescription opioid overdose deaths relative to the amount prescribed, primarily due to its accumulation pharmacokinetics and its use outside of specialty settings. Signs of methadone overdose are the same as other opioids: extreme sedation, slow or absent breathing, pinpoint pupils, cyanosis, unresponsiveness.

Naloxone reverses methadone toxicity but requires repeated dosing — naloxone's duration (30–90 minutes) is much shorter than methadone's half-life. Multiple naloxone doses and hospital observation are essential for methadone overdose management.

Help Is Available: SAMHSA OTP locator: dpt2.samhsa.gov — find a certified methadone treatment clinic near you. SAMHSA Helpline: 1-800-662-4357.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is methadone used for?
Methadone is used for two distinct purposes: (1) treatment of opioid use disorder through methadone maintenance therapy (MMT) at certified opioid treatment programs, and (2) management of moderate to severe chronic pain, particularly when other opioids have failed.
Why is methadone dangerous?
Methadone's extremely long and unpredictable half-life (8–59 hours) means it accumulates in the body over days. A patient may feel fine after initial doses but develop life-threatening respiratory depression on days 3–5 as plasma levels peak. Dose escalation must be very slow, and methadone's use requires significant prescriber expertise.
Can you get methadone for pain without going to a clinic?
Yes. Methadone for pain can be prescribed by any DEA-licensed physician and dispensed at a regular pharmacy like any other Schedule II opioid. It does not require an OTP clinic. However, methadone for opioid use disorder must be dispensed through a federally certified OTP clinic with daily supervised dosing.
What is the lethal dose of methadone?
There is no single lethal dose — risk depends on the individual's opioid tolerance, other medications, and co-ingestions. In opioid-naive individuals, even 30–40 mg can cause fatal respiratory depression. This is why methadone treatment for OUD must start at low doses (20–30 mg) with witnessed administration.
How long does methadone stay in your system?
Methadone has a half-life of 24–36 hours (range 8–59 hours). Detection: urine 3–5 days (some sensitive tests up to 7 days); blood 24–36 hours; hair up to 90 days.
